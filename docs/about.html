The through-line

For fifteen years I ran field-service and distribution operations: a pool service company I owned and operated, and before that a distributor where I was general manager. Those were data jobs before I knew how to write the code: SKU-level demand forecasts, vendor scorecards, technician metrics, route rules and daily KPI dashboards, built in spreadsheets and ERP exports. I made payroll on whether they were right.

  • Blue Turtle Pool Co., 2018 to 2026: every job, part, travel leg and incident logged as a dataset, with dashboards for utilization, response time, recurring revenue and churn.
  • Pool Pros of Hollywood, 2012 to 2022: order, receiving, pick, pack, ship and inventory events in one analytics dataset, with SKU forecasting, reorder rules and vendor scorecards.

I have never had the luxury of an analysis being interesting but wrong.

Where the results come from

  • 20 percent less travel time: routing and schedule rules, Blue Turtle Pool Co.
  • 30 percent fewer emergency parts buys: inventory and seasonal demand forecasts from service logs, Blue Turtle Pool Co.
  • 25 percent fewer stockouts and rush freight: SKU-level forecasts and automated reorder rules, Pool Pros of Hollywood.
  • 10 percent lower cost of goods: seasonal buys with volume and early-buy discounts, Pool Pros of Hollywood.

A plain word on degrees

I do not have a CS or statistics degree. My training is self-directed and ongoing: SQL and Machine Learning from IBM, Python from the University of Michigan, Data Analytics and Data Analysis with R Studio from Google, and the Google Analytics certification.

What I have instead is fifteen years of owning the consequences of an operational number being wrong, and working systems I built and maintain.

How I build pipelines

I build and run a private multi-sport projections platform whose daily pipelines run unattended.

It runs on rules learned the expensive way: append-only writes, explicit casts on every column, load-timestamped run logs, and calibration checks that fail loudly rather than quietly.

Where the analysis shows

Monte Carlo simulation, XGBoost and CatBoost, Gaussian mixture models for segmentation, Bayesian priors for calibration, and structural causal models when the question is whether a signal is real or only correlated.

The Broward Airbnb series is the clearest example of the whole loop: raw Inside Airbnb snapshots in R, metrics in SQL, analysis in Python, and a written answer a non-technical investor can act on.
